# Learning the Internet: competence, autonomy and RTFM

The Internet of the 1990s was not only a new technology. It also had a highly recognizable technical culture and its own social rules.

Much of the network had been built and inhabited by engineers, computer scientists, system administrators, researchers and hackers. In that environment, **competence had value**: understanding how something worked, being able to read documentation, experimenting, and reaching a solution independently were respected abilities.

The other side of this culture was little tolerance for intellectual laziness. Asking a question without first searching, reading or trying to understand the problem was considered poor behavior, especially when the answer was already available in a manual, FAQ, RFC, `man` page, mailing-list archive or Usenet discussion.

## Read first, ask later

The normal path was often something like this:

1. read the manual;
2. consult the `man` pages;
3. search FAQs and RFCs;
4. inspect examples and existing configurations;
5. try;
6. observe what failed;
7. only then ask for help.

A question showing that this work had already been done could receive remarkably knowledgeable and generous answers. A question showing that it had not could receive a much shorter reply.

## RTFM

**RTFM** meant:

> **Read The Fucking Manual.**

It was a typical response when someone asked a question whose answer was already clearly explained in available documentation.

The expression was deliberately blunt, but the principle behind it was precise: **before asking other people to invest their time and expertise, you were expected to invest your own time in searching, reading, trying and understanding**.

It was therefore not merely sarcasm. It was also a community norm.

The culture is documented in contemporary sources. RFC 1855, *Netiquette Guidelines*, published in October 1995, advised users to consult manuals, help systems and other reference material before asking questions, and noted that questions whose answers were readily available could provoke irritated responses. The *Jargon File* recorded RTFM as part of the technical vocabulary of hacker and Unix culture.

- RFC 1855, *Netiquette Guidelines*: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1855
- *Jargon File*, “RTFM”: https://catb.org/jargon/html/R/RTFM.html

## Learning by building

Internet Force was created inside this culture.

There was no packaged training path explaining how to build an Internet service provider. Much of the learning happened by working directly with:

- Unix and Sun manuals;
- RFCs and protocol documentation;
- `man` pages;
- product documentation;
- source code and configuration files;
- mailing lists and Usenet;
- direct experimentation on systems;
- exchanges with other technical people.

Documentation was not something consulted after the course: very often, **it was the course**.

This way of learning also helps explain the material preserved in this archive. Configuration files are not merely artifacts: they show a culture in which understanding systems meant opening them, reading them, changing them, breaking them, rebuilding them and directly observing their behavior.

## Personal note

Thirty years later, I do not regard RTFM merely as a relic of a rougher technical culture. Behind that expression was a principle I still consider valid: before asking others to invest their time and expertise, you were expected to invest your own time in searching, reading, trying and understanding.

That culture could certainly be unforgiving. But I believe contemporary culture has moved too far in the opposite direction: fear of causing offence and growing oversensitivity make it harder to say openly that not every question deserves the same response, and that personal effort in learning and investigating matters.

**RTFM was brutal. The principle behind it was much less so.**
